We taught people to code. Now we teach them to think.

In 2024, after nearly a decade of running coding bootcamps, Re:Coded did something unusual for a nonprofit: we decided to stop. Not because the work had failed — it hadn’t — but because we were no longer sure we were solving the right problem.

Alexandra ClareCEO & Co-Founder28 September 20265 min read

When Re:Coded launched our first coding bootcamp in 2016, the logic felt airtight. Young people in conflict-affected communities needed jobs. The tech industry needed developers. We would build the bridge. And for a while, it worked beautifully. Thousands of graduates. An 87% job placement rate. Recognition from Google, the UN, CNN, and the BBC. Learning to code was a reliable passport to economic opportunity, and junior developer roles were plentiful. We were proud of what we built, and we still are.

But as AI gained traction, something started to shift. Junior developer hiring began to slow. The tasks that once defined entry-level tech roles like boilerplate code, basic debugging and routine content production started to become increasingly automated. The career ladder our graduates were climbing was being restructured beneath them in real time.

Meanwhile, the funding landscape was shifting beneath us too. Donor attention was moving toward defence, climate, and geopolitical crisis response. The decade-long investment in refugee education and workforce development that had made organisations like ours possible was quietly contracting. We were facing not just a pedagogical question but an existential one.

The honest conversation we needed to have

By 2024, we started to see cracks both in what we were teaching and in the broader developmental landscape. And so, over the course of six months, we started to ask ourselves a very necessary but uncomfortable question: are we still solving the right problem? Was it time to wind down our programs and everything we had spent years building?

As hard as it was to answer, we had to be honest with ourselves. And the honest answer was that it was time to compost what we had built. Letting go was hard. But the space it created was clarifying.

Over the course of a year, we said goodbye not knowing what would come next. For the first time in years, we had the time to sit back, read, and think seriously about the forces shaping our futures: climate change, AI, the reconfiguration of global labour markets among many other forces. We started to ask a different question — not what skills are in demand right now, but what capabilities do people genuinely need to stay resilient in a world that will keep changing faster than any curriculum can keep up with?

The answer was not simply more technical training. It was something harder to teach and more durable once learned.

It is the ability to think. The ability to understand systems. The ability to frame a problem before rushing to solve it. Giving and receiving feedback with honesty and grace. Communicating clearly across distance and difference. Understanding how AI tools actually work — and crucially, how they fail, so you can use them with judgment rather than blind trust.

These are not nice-to-haves. They are, increasingly, the differentiating capabilities that determine whether a young person can build a resilient career in an AI-assisted workplace or whether they become dependent on tools they do not understand, producing outputs they cannot evaluate.

For young people in conflict-affected and low-income communities, the stakes are especially high. They cannot afford to spend years building skills that are obsolete before they have had the chance to deploy them. And they deserve an education that treats them as the critical thinkers and future leaders they are not simply as recipients of technical training designed for a world that no longer exists.

What this looks like in practice

This thinking is reflected in our two newest programs. The Resilient Futures Fellowship is a four-month leadership program for emerging technologists and entrepreneurs, combining AI ethics, digital entrepreneurship, and systems thinking with mentorship and real-world project development. And our newest program, Source Code Accelerator, starts on 20 November: a free, seven-week online course for software developers in Iraq. But it looks different from anything we have run before.

Week one is not about code. It is about work itself — what it is for, how to manage your energy across unstable conditions, how to say no with an alternative, how to build a sustainable professional life in a world that is anything but stable.

Week two is about communication and collaboration — how to write clearly, give feedback that lands, disagree with a manager without burning a relationship, and work effectively with people you will never meet in person.

Week three is about thinking in systems — how to frame a problem before reaching for a solution, how to spot the second and third-order consequences of a decision, how to tell the difference between a complicated problem and a complex one.

Only in week four do we turn to AI directly. And when we do, we do not teach participants to be enthusiastic users of these tools. We teach them how language models actually work — and why they hallucinate confidently, why they cannot signal uncertainty, and why a system that is right 90% of the time with no indication of which 10% is a fundamentally different engineering challenge from one that is right 99% of the time. We cover the Arabic-language performance gaps in leading models that directly affect anyone building for users in our region. We take the risks seriously.

A word about what we are not saying

To be clear: we have not given up on technical skills. AI tools require technical judgment to use well, and our graduates will be more technically capable, not less. What we are saying is that technical skills alone stripped of the human capabilities that give them direction are no longer sufficient. The most important investment we can make is not handing young people a toolkit, but helping them become the kind of thinkers who can adapt as the toolkit changes.

The future we are working toward

Re:Coded was founded on the belief that talent is equally distributed but opportunity is not. That belief has not changed. What has changed is our understanding of what opportunity actually requires in 2026 and beyond.

It requires AI fluency — honest, grounded, critical fluency, not hype. It requires the human capabilities that no model can replicate: curiosity, judgment, ethical reasoning, the ability to work alongside other human beings with empathy and integrity. And it requires the resilience to navigate a world that will continue to change faster than any curriculum can keep up with.

That is what we are building toward. Not graduates who can pass a technical test, but people who are genuinely prepared for whatever comes next. We do this with learners from 145 countries and with the hard-won knowledge that the most radical thing we can offer is not a shortcut to employment but the depth of thinking that makes employment, and everything beyond it, sustainable.

The bootcamp taught people to code. What we are building now teaches people to think. In an age of AI, we believe that is the most radical thing we can offer.
